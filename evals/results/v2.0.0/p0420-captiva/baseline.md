## What P0420 means

**P0420 = "Catalyst System Efficiency Below Threshold (Bank 1)."** The engine computer compares the oxygen sensor before the catalytic converter with the one after it. A healthy converter smooths out the downstream sensor's signal. When the downstream signal starts to look like the upstream one, the computer decides the converter isn't cleaning the exhaust well enough and sets the code.

## Is it safe to drive?

**Usually yes, in the short term.** P0420 alone is an emissions fault, not an immediate mechanical danger. Stop driving and get it checked soon if you notice any of these:

- **A flashing check-engine light.** This means an active misfire, and unburned fuel can overheat and destroy the converter quickly.
- **Loss of power, or the engine struggles on hills or at highway speed.** This can mean the converter is clogged and restricting exhaust.
- **A rotten-egg or sulfur smell, or rattling from under the car.** These point to a failing or broken-up converter.
- **Any other codes alongside it**, such as P0300–P0306 (misfires), O2 sensor codes, or fuel trim codes P0171/P0172.

If the light is steady and the car drives normally, you can keep driving while you sort it out. You will fail an emissions inspection, though, and the check-engine light won't be able to warn you about a new problem while it's already on.

## Common causes (cheapest to check first)

1. **Exhaust leak** near the O2 sensors, such as a cracked manifold or bad gasket. This can trigger a false P0420.
2. **A failing downstream (or upstream) O2 sensor.** The part costs about $40–100.
3. **Misfires or a rich-running engine** damaging the converter: worn plugs or coils, leaky injectors.
4. **Oil burning.** This one matters on your car. The 2014 Captiva's 2.4L Ecotec (LE5) is known for excessive oil consumption from stuck piston rings. Burned oil coats and kills catalytic converters. The 3.0L V6 has its own oil and timing-chain issues.
5. **A worn-out catalytic converter.** This is common on a 12-year-old car with high mileage.

## What to do

1. **Check your oil level now.** If it's regularly low between changes, oil burning is probably the root cause. A new converter will fail again unless that's addressed.
2. **Scan for other codes.** Many auto parts stores do this for free.
3. **Have a shop check for exhaust leaks and look at live O2 sensor data** before replacing anything. A downstream sensor that switches rapidly, like the upstream one, confirms the converter is the problem.
4. **If the converter is bad:**
   - An aftermarket unit usually runs about $400–1,200 installed.
   - An OEM unit costs more.
   - In California and other CARB states you'll need a CARB-compliant unit, which costs more.
   - Check whether your Captiva's emissions warranty still applies. The federal warranty on major emissions parts is 8 years/80,000 miles, so on a 2014 it has very likely expired, but it's worth a quick look.

Avoid "cat cleaner" additives and O2 sensor spacers sold as fixes. At best they hide the problem, and spacers may not be legal for emissions compliance.
