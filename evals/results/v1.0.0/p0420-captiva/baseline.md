**P0420 means "Catalyst System Efficiency Below Threshold (Bank 1)."** Your engine computer compares the oxygen sensor before the catalytic converter with the one after it. A healthy converter smooths out the exhaust, so the downstream sensor reads fairly steady. When the downstream sensor starts switching almost as much as the upstream one, the computer decides the converter isn't cleaning the exhaust well enough and sets the code.

## Is it safe to drive?

Usually yes, in the short term. P0420 by itself isn't an emergency, and many people drive for weeks with it. Watch for these warning signs, though:

- **Flashing check engine light.** That means an active misfire, which dumps unburned fuel into the converter and can overheat it. Stop driving hard and get it looked at soon.
- **Rattling under the car**, especially at startup. The converter's internal honeycomb may be breaking apart.
- **Rotten egg smell, loss of power, or the engine struggling at highway speed.** These can point to a clogged converter restricting exhaust flow.
- **Other codes alongside it**, especially misfire codes (P0300 to P0304), O2 sensor codes, or fuel trim codes (P0171/P0172). Those are often the real cause.

If none of those apply, you can keep driving while you sort it out. The main practical problem is that you'll fail an emissions inspection, and the lit check engine light will hide any new problems that come up.

## Common causes on a 2014 Captiva

1. **Engine burning oil (2.4L engine).** This is the big one for this vehicle. The 2.4L Ecotec from this era is known for piston ring problems that cause oil consumption. Burned oil coats and poisons the catalytic converter over time. If you're adding oil between changes, this is likely what's behind the code, and replacing the converter without fixing the oil burning means the new one will fail too. GM issued extended coverage for this on some vehicles, so it's worth asking a dealer whether your VIN qualifies.
2. **Worn catalytic converter.** At 10+ years old, this could simply be age.
3. **Faulty downstream O2 sensor.** Cheaper to replace and worth ruling out first.
4. **Exhaust leak** near the sensors, which can throw off the readings.
5. **Misfires or a rich fuel condition** that damaged the converter or are confusing the sensors.

## What I'd do next

1. **Check your oil level** and think about how often you've been topping it off.
2. **Look at freeze frame data and live O2 sensor readings** with a scan tool. If the downstream sensor (Bank 1 Sensor 2) is switching rapidly like the upstream one, the converter is likely tired. If it's flat or lazy, the sensor itself may be the issue.
3. **Check for exhaust leaks** around the manifold and converter.
4. **Clear the code and see if it returns.** Sometimes a single bad drive cycle sets it. If it comes back within a week or two, it's real.
5. **Get a quote before replacing the converter.** Aftermarket converters run roughly $300 to $800 installed, while OEM parts can push past $1,500. Make sure it's an EPA or CARB compliant unit depending on your state's rules.

Skip the "catalytic converter cleaner" bottles and O2 sensor spacers. The cleaners rarely fix a truly worn converter, and spacers just mask the problem.
